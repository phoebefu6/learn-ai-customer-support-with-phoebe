Part 1 · covers Erlang C and the service level

Agents do not scale with volume

7 min live

Halve the contacts and you do not halve the agents. Customers arrive at random, so some minutes bring three chats at once, and a queue that is busy 95 percent of the time keeps almost everyone waiting. Erlang C turns arrivals and handle time into the number of people needed to answer most customers quickly.

LiveErlang C in four sentences, and 80/20 in one4 min▶

Workload is arrivals times handle time: 100 calls a quarter hour at 3.5 minutes each is about 23.3 agents of continuous work. Erlang C gives the probability that an arriving customer has to wait at all, for a given number of agents above that workload; the ITU-D handbook calls it "Erlang's C-formula, Erlang's second formula, or Erlang's formula for waiting time systems". The service-level formula then gives the share answered within t seconds: one minus that probability times a decay that depends on the spare agents. Staffing is the smallest number of agents that reaches the target.

The target Quayside uses is the common one. Ger Koole defines it as "the minimal number of agents required to answer 80% of the calls within 20 seconds". In his worked example, Erlang C says 24 agents answer only about 21 percent that fast and 28 reach 80 percent. The bench's implementation gives 20.6 percent at 24 and 83.0 at 28.

Real world

This is the arithmetic behind every "the bot frees up X agents" claim. Containment removes contacts; Erlang C decides how many agents those contacts were costing. Remove the easy, quick contacts and the remaining queue keeps its peaks, which is why the bench's vendor pitch removes 70 percent of first contacts and only a third of the agents.

Self-studyTwo things the bench's Erlang C leaves out4 min▶
  • Abandonment. Koole: "some calls, while waiting for service in queue, abandon", which is "not part of the Erlang C model". Ignoring it overstates the agents needed when queues are long. Koole calls the model that includes it Erlang X; the academic literature also uses the name Erlang A, which was not read at source here.
  • Chat concurrency. The bench staffs chat as one conversation per agent at a time. Agents who hold two or three chats at once need fewer seats and take longer per chat. If your floor works concurrently, the shape of every result below holds and the agent counts do not.

Email is not queued in real time, so the bench adds it as workload hours spread over the day rather than running it through Erlang C.

Part 2 · covers the true-resolution funnel and cost per resolved contact

Follow 14,000 contacts to the ones that were actually solved

6 min live

The bench takes session 1's definitions and runs them on a month. Every contact either reaches a person, or is resolved by the bot, or is falsely contained. The falsely contained either come back, at a higher handle time, or never come back. Cost per resolved contact divides what the month cost by the customers whose problem ended.

LiveTwo costs per resolved contact, and why you report both4 min▶

Operating cost per resolved contact is agents plus AI fees, divided by customers whose problem ended. All-in adds one more line: the customers a false containment loses. Quayside's constructed assumption is that 15 percent of the customers who never come back close their account, each worth $120 a year. Churn modelling proper belongs to Customer Retention ↗; here it is one honest, labelled line.

The two disagree exactly where the decision is. On the bench, pushing the bot to 90 percent has the lowest operating cost of any setting, $5.67, and an all-in cost of $6.60, worse than the plan's $6.29, with 1,722 customers a month told they were done. Report one number and you choose the plan by accident.

Real world

Klarna's CEO, reported by CX Dive quoting a May 2025 Bloomberg interview: "As cost unfortunately seems to have been a too predominant evaluation factor when organizing this, what you end up having is lower quality". Session 5 showed the other side of that story, which Klarna also tells. The bench cannot settle it; it can show you which of your own numbers would have warned you.

Self-studyPricing per outcome, per conversation, per token3 min▶

Intercom lists Fin at "$0.99 per outcome" and counts an assumed resolution as an outcome. Salesforce's pricing page lists "$2" per conversation for Agentforce, alongside Flex Credits. The bench charges a flat price per bot conversation and lets you set it: at $2 instead of $0.99, Quayside's plan moves from $6.17 to $6.53 per resolved contact. Under an outcome model you also pay for false containments that were billed as outcomes, which is part of the false-containment cost the bench reports.

The cost of a single model call, in tokens, is one line inside that price and is taught in LLM Inference: where the money goes ↗. Vendor prices change often: re-verify before you sign.

Build-along

The bench: price Quayside's plan, then write the memo

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The bench below computes everything live as you press a rung or move a lever. Walk the ladder in order; every number in the steps is one the bench prints. Then build your own plan, and write the memo from it.

Press "Today, no AI". 24 agents, peak 7 seats at 80/20, $108,000 a month, $7.71 per resolved contact. This is the baseline every other rung is compared with, and it matches the 24 agents Quayside has.

Press "The vendor pitch: 70%". 16 agents. Tick "Show the vendor dashboard" and the bot's resolution reads 100.0 percent; untick it and the true figure is 60.0 percent. 3,920 customers a month falsely contained, 1,568 of them never come back. Operating cost $6.57 per resolved contact, and $8.84 all-in: worse than today.

Press "Assist only". No bot at all, agent assist at the published 14 percent: 23 agents and $7.39. Modest, and with zero falsely contained customers.

Press "Automate the safe third", then "+ a hand-off". The bot takes 35 percent, the share session 2's gated map could answer safely: 19 agents, 735 falsely contained, $6.98 all-in. A hand-off that catches 70 percent of the bot's failing chats leaves 221 falsely contained and $6.61 all-in. The dashboard now reads 89.5 percent against a true 85.0, because hand-offs are no longer counted as resolved.

Press "The plan". Safe third, hand-off, and assist: 18 agents, $85,851 a month, $6.17 per resolved contact, $6.29 all-in, 221 falsely contained, 88 never come back.

Press "Push to 90%". 14 agents and the lowest operating cost on the bench, $5.67. All-in $6.60, worse than the plan, with 1,722 falsely contained and true bot resolution down to 54.4 percent. Then press "Same plan at $2": $6.53 per resolved contact instead of $6.17.

Now make your own. Move the levers until you have a plan you would sign, then press "Write Quayside's plan memo from these numbers". It unlocks at 4 passport stamps, and the link beside it opens it anyway. Copy the memo into section 6.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the bench, the vendor pitch cuts agents from 24 to 16. Why is its all-in cost per resolved contact, $8.84, higher than today's $7.71?

Operating cost per resolved contact does fall, to $6.57. The all-in figure adds what false containment costs, and at a true resolution of 60.0 percent that is most of the saving and more.

2 · In Koole's worked example, 24 agents answer about 21 percent of calls within 20 seconds and 28 answer 80 percent. What does that teach about staffing?

Workload was about 23.3 agents; staffing it at 24 leaves almost no slack, and waits explode. That non-linearity is why the bench runs Erlang C hour by hour instead of scaling headcount by containment.

3 · "Push to 90%" has the lowest operating cost on the bench, $5.67. Why does the plan at 35 percent still win?

Past the safe third, the bot is answering questions it cannot resolve. The second number on the bench, customers falsely contained, is what keeps the first from choosing for you.
