LiveWhy the middle tier gets its own session3 min▶

Most of the AI conversation in support is about the bot: what it can answer alone. The assist tier is quieter and, on the evidence, better understood. It is where the customer needs something only their account can answer (where is my card, why is my transfer pending), so a person must answer, and the question becomes how much faster and better that person gets with a draft in front of them.

It is also where a support plan most often goes wrong without anyone noticing, because an assistant that is trusted too much is a bot with an extra click. Tonight's rules are about keeping that click meaningful.

Real world

Session 2 quoted one line from the study this session is built on: "the gains from AI adoption are largest for moderately rare problems" (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, QJE 2025 abstract). Rare problems are poor automation candidates and good assist candidates. That is the bridge from the map to tonight.

Part 1 · covers agent assist vs a customer-facing bot

Who presses send is the whole difference

6 min live

The same model, the same help centre and the same answer can be a bot or an assistant. What decides it is one thing: whether a person stands between the answer and the customer. In a bot loop, nobody does. In an assist loop, the agent reads, edits or rejects, and only then sends.

LiveWho presses send3 min▶

Put any AI feature in a support stack through one question: when the customer reads the reply, did a person decide to send it? If yes, it is assist. If no, it is a bot, whatever the vendor calls it, and everything in session 1 about containment and resolution applies to it.

Customer-facing botAgent assist
Who reads the AI's text firstThe customerThe agent

The quiet failure. An assistant becomes a bot the day agents stop reading the draft. Nothing in the software changes; the edit rate falls to zero and nobody notices. That is why the build-along logs every edit.

Real world

Klarna's February 2024 announcement described an assistant that handled 2.3 million conversations, "two-thirds of Klarna's customer service chats", in its first month. It answered customers directly: a bot loop. The study in Part 2 is the opposite design: a tool that gave suggestions to agents, who could use them or ignore them. Both get called "AI in customer service". They are different decisions with different evidence, and session 5 takes the Klarna numbers apart.

Self-studyWhich of Quayside's intents are assist4 min▶

Session 2's map put 29 of Quayside's 77 intents in the assist tier (constructed tiers, applied to real Banking77 messages). The test that put them there was answerability: answering needs the customer's own account, so the help centre alone cannot do it. Card arrival and a pending transfer are the typical shape.

Two consequences follow for tonight's rules. First, the draft can only be as good as what the assistant is allowed to see: a draft written from the help centre alone will be generic for exactly these intents, and the agent supplies the account-specific part. Second, the 12 human-tier intents (fraud, disputes, identity, closing an account) are not assist intents. A draft there is a temptation, not a help, and Quayside's rules give them none.

Check your own map. For each assist intent, write what the agent looks up in the account before replying. If the answer is "nothing", the intent may belong in automate. If it is "something the customer could be harmed by", it may belong in human.

Part 2 · covers the strongest evidence on assist

What one study of 5,000 agents found, in two versions

6 min live

Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond studied a generative AI assistant rolled out to customer support agents at a Fortune 500 software firm. The paper exists as a 2023 NBER working paper and a 2025 journal article, and the two abstracts do not say quite the same thing. The difference matters most for the group you might assume benefits most.

LiveThe two versions, side by side4 min▶
NBER Working Paper 31161 (2023)Quarterly Journal of Economics (2025)
Agents5,1795,172
Average gain14% in issues resolved per hour15%, "with substantial heterogeneity across workers"
Newer, lower-skilled agents"a 34% improvement for novice and low-skilled workers""improve both the speed and quality of their output"
Most experienced, most skilled"minimal impact""small gains in speed and small declines in quality"

The QJE abstract adds two findings the agents' managers will care about: "customers are more polite and less likely to ask to speak to a manager", and "the gains from AI adoption are largest for moderately rare problems". The working paper's abstract reports that assistance "improves customer sentiment, increases employee retention, and may lead to worker learning", and the body of the arXiv version says attrition fell, "driven by the retention of newer workers".

Cite the version you quote. "34 percent" is true of the NBER abstract. The journal version's text puts the low-skill gain at about 30 percent, and changes the story for the most skilled from no effect to a small quality decline. Anyone who quotes 34 percent as "the AI productivity gain" has picked the largest subgroup number from the earlier version.

Self-studyWhat the study can and cannot tell Quayside4 min▶

What it was. Agent assist: the tool suggested responses and the human sent. It was not a customer-facing bot, so it says nothing about containment or false containment. The setting was "a Fortune 500 software firm that provides business process software", and most agents "work from offices located in the Philippines" (arXiv v2).

What transfers. The shape: the largest gains go to the people with the least experience, on the problems they have seen least. The direction for the most skilled: little speed to gain, and some quality to lose, at least in the journal version.

What does not transfer automatically. The size. Quayside is a constructed bank, with different products, a different help centre and a different mix of tenure. One study in one firm is the strongest evidence available, not a forecast for yours. That is why the build-along caps the planning number rather than copying the headline.

Real world

The heterogeneity is the finding. The same tool, in the same firm, made the least experienced agents faster and better, and made the most skilled agents slightly worse on quality. A rollout plan that treats every agent the same is ignoring the main result.

Part 3 · covers grounding the draft

A draft you can check in seconds shows where it came from

5 min live

The agent's check is only fast if the draft carries its evidence. A grounded draft quotes the help-centre article it used and links to it, so the agent compares two lines instead of searching for one. When no article is found, the honest draft is no draft: the agent answers alone.

LiveThe citation rule, and the empty case3 min▶

Two rules make a draft checkable. Every factual sentence in the draft quotes or points to the help-centre article it came from, with a link. And if no article supports an answer, the assistant shows no draft and says so: "No article found. Answer yourself."

The second rule is the one teams skip. A fluent draft with no source is the most dangerous thing an assistant can produce, because it reads exactly like a grounded one and the agent has nothing to check it against. An empty panel costs the agent a few seconds of writing. A confident invented fee costs a customer.

How retrieval and citation are actually built (chunking, ranking, citation formats, refusing when nothing matches) is taught in full in RAG: grounding and citations ↗ and, for an internal helpdesk, Ship an Internal AI Assistant: grounded answers ↗. Tonight decides only what the support plan requires of the draft.

Self-studyWhy the check has to be fast2 min▶

The study in Part 2 measured issues resolved per hour. Every second the agent spends verifying a draft comes out of that gain. If checking an ungrounded draft means opening the help centre and searching, a careful agent loses the time the draft saved, and a hurried agent stops checking. Grounding is what lets the agent be both fast and careful.

Real world

Zendesk's "Verified" resolution tier, introduced in May 2026, has a large language model that "evaluates the text of the conversation to confirm that the customer's request was satisfactorily resolved". That is a machine reading the conversation after the fact. A grounded draft puts a person's read before the fact, and the citation is what makes that read take seconds.

Build-along

Quayside's assist rules, written down

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Six decisions, each one a line in section 3 of Quayside's plan. Write them as you go; the last step assembles them. Then score the plan so far on the halfway scorecard. No tool needed.

Decide which intents get drafts. Only the assist tier from session 2's map: 29 intents that need the customer's account. Automate-tier intents go to the bot; human-tier intents get no draft at all, because a ready-made reply on a fraud or dispute case is exactly the shortcut a tired agent should not be offered.

Scope Drafts appear on the 29 assist intents only. No drafts on human-tier intents (fraud, disputes, identity, closing an account).

Write the citation rule. Every factual sentence in a draft links the help-centre article it came from. No article, no draft: the panel says so and the agent writes the reply.

Grounding Every draft quotes and links the help-centre article it used. If no article supports an answer, show no draft: "No article found. Answer yourself."

Write who may send. The agent, always. No auto-send on a timer, no "send if confidence is high", no exception for quiet hours. The moment an assistant can send by itself it is a bot, and it belongs back in session 1's metric and session 2's automate tier.

Who sends The agent sends every reply. The assistant never sends, on any intent, at any hour, at any confidence.

Decide who sees drafts, and when. Newer agents get a suggestion on every chat, because that is where the study found both speed and quality improve. The most experienced agents get drafts on demand, because the journal version reports "small declines in quality" for them. The study did not test an on-demand setting; this is Quayside's reading of its heterogeneity, and session 5's QA sample is where you would see if it is wrong.

Who sees drafts Newer agents: a draft on every assist chat. Most experienced agents: drafts on demand (one click). Review this split after the first QA read in session 5.

Log every edit. Store the draft and the reply that was actually sent, side by side, for every assist chat. The edit rate per intent tells you where the help centre is thin; an edit rate falling to zero tells you agents have stopped reading.

Edit log For every assist chat, keep: intent, draft, sent reply, edited yes / no, rejected yes / no, agent tenure band. Feeds session 5's QA sample.

Cap the planning number. Plan on +14 percent resolutions per hour: the NBER average, and the lower of the two published averages. Not 34 percent, which is one subgroup in one version. Session 6's simulator caps the assist lever at exactly this number. On that bench, assist alone takes Quayside from 24 agents to 23, and cost per resolved contact from $7.71 to $7.39 (session 6 bench, constructed inputs).

Constructed Quayside example.

Draft, quoting "Card delivery": Cards usually arrive within a few working days. If yours has not arrived, you can order a replacement in the app.

Grounded and polite, and wrong for this customer: it answers the general question, not theirs.

Sent by the agent: Your card came back to us undelivered, so a replacement would have gone to the same address. I have updated the address you confirmed and reissued the card.

The agent checked the account, kept the tone, replaced the advice. One logged edit.

Message. "My new card still hasn't come." An assist intent: the answer is in the account.

Draft. The assistant quotes the card delivery article and links it.

Check. The agent opens the link and the account. The article is right; the account says the card came back.

Edit. The agent rewrites the advice. The log keeps draft and reply side by side.

Send. The agent presses send. The assistant never does.

The metric definition is written down with a window A named number of days for a repeat contact, not "we track repeats". Half marks if the window exists but nobody owns it.
Containment and true resolution are reported side by side Both numbers in the same weekly line. Half marks if true resolution is computed but lives in a different report.
The map was drawn risk before volume Money, identity and regulated intents went to a person first; volume came last. Half marks if you applied it to most intents but not all.
A gate was chosen with its trade-off written down A confidence threshold plus what it costs: the coverage you gave up and the wrong answers you still accept. Half marks for a threshold with no stated cost.
The assist rules say the agent always sends No auto-send, on any intent, at any confidence. Half marks if there is one exception you can defend in writing.

Halfway, not final. This scorecard is forgiving on purpose: it checks that sections 1 to 3 exist and say the right thing. Session 6 has a strict one for the whole plan, priced. Anything you score 0 tonight is the cheapest thing to fix before then.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Run the send test on your own stack. List every AI feature your team uses or is evaluating. For each, answer: when the customer reads the reply, did a person decide to send it? Mark each one bot or assist.
  2. Find the version. Find one slide, article or vendor deck that quotes a productivity number for AI in support. Trace it to its source and write down which version and which subgroup it comes from.
  3. Read ten drafts against their sources. If you have an assistant, pick ten drafts and check each against the article it cites. Count how many had no source, and how many cited an article that did not say what the draft said.
  4. Start the edit log. Even by hand for one day: for ten assist chats, copy the draft and the reply that was sent. Mark which were edited. Bring the count to session 5.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · According to Generative AI at Work, what happened to the most experienced and highest-skilled agents with the AI assistant?

34 percent is the NBER figure for novice and low-skilled agents only. For the most skilled, the working paper reports "minimal impact" and the journal version reports "small gains in speed and small declines in quality". Cite the version you quote.

2 · Why do Quayside's assist rules say the agent always presses send, with no auto-send even at high confidence?

Who presses send is the whole difference between assist and a bot. The evidence in Part 2 is about agents who could ignore suggestions; an auto-send removes the agent's check and the evidence with it.

3 · Quayside's plan caps the assist lever at +14 percent resolutions per hour. Why not 34 percent?

Quayside has a mix of agents, so the planning number is the average, and the lower of the two published averages (14 in NBER 2023, 15 in QJE 2025). Nothing was retracted, and Quayside is constructed: it has no pilot to measure.
