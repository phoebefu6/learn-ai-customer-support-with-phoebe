The hand-off: let go before the loop

Every bot fails some customers. The difference between a plan that works and one that ends up in front of a tribunal is what happens next: whether something notices, whether a person takes over, and whether that person already knows what the customer said. Tonight you write the rules for that moment, section 4 of Quayside's support operating plan.

LiveTwo messages the map could not stop3 min▶

In session 2's playground, two real customer messages, "Why can't I verify my identity?" and "It is impossible to verify my identity", were both scored 0.68 as why verify identity, a harmless explainer. Their true label is unable to verify identity: a customer locked out of their own account. No sensible confidence gate catches a 0.68.

So the bot answers. It explains why banks verify identity. The customer writes back. That second turn is where the hand-off earns its place: the classifier was confidently wrong once, and the conversation itself is now the evidence.

Part 1 · covers hand-off triggers

Five triggers, checked on every turn

7 min live

A hand-off rule that is only checked on the first message is a routing rule. A real one runs on every customer turn, because most of what goes wrong in a bot conversation is only visible after the bot has answered once.

LiveThe five triggers, and what each one catches4 min▶
TriggerWhat it catchesWhen it is checked
Human-tier intentAnything session 2's map put in the human column: lost or stolen cards, charges twice, identity lockoutsFrom the first message
Negative sentiment or alarm words"freeze", "stolen", "complaint", and a customer whose tone has turnedEvery turn
Repeated failureThe bot has failed twice, or the customer rephrases the same questionEvery turn after the first answer
Regulated topicDisputes, complaints, closing an account, source of funds, a vulnerable customerEvery turn
Explicit ask for a person"Can I talk to someone", in any wordingEvery turn, no argument

Notice which trigger catches the identity message. Not the intent: the classifier got that wrong with confidence. Not a threshold: 0.68 passes any sensible gate. Repeated failure catches it, because a customer who asks the same thing twice has told you the first answer did not work. The classifier can be wrong; the conversation is evidence.

Real world

When Gartner surveyed 5,728 customers in December 2023, it reported in July 2024 that the top concern about AI in service was that "it will get more difficult reaching a person". The explicit-ask trigger is the direct answer to that fear, which is why it fires every time, with no attempt to talk the customer out of it.

Self-studyA promise of a person is now part of the pitch3 min▶

In May 2025, Klarna's chief executive Sebastian Siemiatkowski reportedly said that "there will be always a human if you want". This is reported: CX Dive quoted it from Bloomberg, whose page could not be read. The same report quotes him saying that cost had been "a too predominant evaluation factor" and that the result was "lower quality".

Whatever you make of the Klarna story, and session 5 lays out both sides, the sentence is a product promise. A promise of a person is only true if a trigger fires on the ask and an agent is there to take it. That makes trigger 5 a staffing decision as much as a bot setting, and session 6 prices it.

Banking-specific rules on complaints handling and timelines vary by jurisdiction and are not taught here. They appear only as the reason "regulated topic" is a trigger category: when a topic has a legal clock, a person starts it.

Part 2 · covers the context packet

The context packet: nobody asks twice

5 min live

A hand-off that drops the conversation is a second contact wearing a disguise. The customer starts again, more annoyed, with a person who knows less than the bot did. The context packet is what travels with the chat so the agent's first line can be "I can see what happened", not "How can I help?".

LiveSeven fields, and the question each one saves3 min▶
FieldThe question it saves
The customer's own words"Can you describe the problem?" Verbatim, not the bot's summary, because the summary is where a misreading hides
Intent and its score"What is this about?" And it shows the agent when the bot was confidently wrong, like the 0.68
What the bot already saidStops the agent repeating the answer that already failed
What was already verified"Can you confirm your details?" asked a second time
Account references"Which card, which payment?"
SentimentTells the agent to open with an apology rather than a greeting
Why the hand-off firedTells the agent what kind of conversation this is before reading a word of it

In Quayside's constructed model, the one session 6 prices, a customer who has to explain again takes 1.5 times the normal handle time. That multiplier is constructed and labelled so on the bench; your numbers will differ. The direction is not in doubt: every field the packet carries is a minute the agent does not spend rebuilding the conversation.

Real world

The strongest field evidence on AI in support is about agents, not bots. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's study, published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2025, reports that with AI assistance "customers are more polite and less likely to ask to speak to a manager". An agent who starts with the full context is in the same position: the escalation the customer was building towards never needs to happen.

Self-studyThe phone version: warm transfer and consent2 min▶

Everything here is written for chat and email, which carry Quayside's volume. On a call, the hand-off becomes a warm transfer, and the packet has to reach the agent before the customer does, along with the consent notices a recorded or AI-handled call needs. Part 3 · covers the bot loop and who is liable

The loop, and who answers for what the bot says

5 min live

A bot that keeps answering after it has failed is the most expensive moment in the conversation. Each extra turn makes the customer angrier, makes the eventual agent's job longer, and gives the bot one more chance to say something the company will be held to. One tribunal has already said so in writing.

LiveMoffatt v. Air Canada, in five facts4 min▶

The decision was issued on February 14, 2024 by British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal, cited as 2024 BCCRT 149. A customer asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares. The chatbot said:

The chatbot's answer, as quoted in the decision "If you need to travel immediately or have already travelled and would like to submit your ticket for a reduced bereavement rate, kindly do so within 90 days of the date your ticket was issued"

The customer relied on that answer. Air Canada argued, in the tribunal's words, that "the chatbot is a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions. This is a remarkable submission." The tribunal went on: "It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website. It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot."

The finding was negligent misrepresentation: "Air Canada did not take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate." The customer claimed $880 and was awarded $812.02: $650.88 in damages, $36.14 in pre-judgment interest and $125 in tribunal fees, in Canadian dollars.

Real world

This is a small-claims tribunal decision in British Columbia. It is persuasive, not binding precedent anywhere else, and the amount is small. It matters anyway, because the reasoning is the one any customer, regulator or journalist will reach for first: the chatbot is the company, speaking. Read it in full at the tribunal's own site: Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149 ↗.

Self-studyWhat the decision means for the hand-off3 min▶

Three operating consequences follow, none of them legal advice:

  • Policy questions with money attached are a trigger, not a topic. Refunds, fees, eligibility: if a wrong answer is a promise the company may be held to, the bot hands off or quotes the policy page word for word.
  • The bot's last message is a statement too. What the bot says while handing over must never promise an outcome on money or policy. "A colleague will look at your refund" is safe; "you will get your refund" is a commitment.
  • The loop multiplies exposure. Every extra turn after the bot has failed is another chance to say something like the sentence above. Repeated failure is the trigger that closes the loop.

Whether the bot's answers are grounded in the help centre in the first place belongs to session 3's assist rules and to RAG: grounding and citations ↗. The hand-off is the safety net under that, not a replacement for it.

Build-along

Quayside's hand-off rules

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You now write section 4 of Quayside's plan: which triggers, at what thresholds, on which turn, what the packet carries, what the bot says on the way out, and who owns it. Every threshold below is a constructed Quayside choice. Change any of them you can defend differently.

List the triggers with thresholds. Start from the five in Part 1 and give each one a number or a list you could hand to whoever configures the bot. "Repeated failure" is not a rule until it says how many turns.

★ Triggers and thresholds (constructed) Human-tier intent: any of the 12 on session 2's map Alarm words: "freeze", "stolen", "complaint", "fraud" Repeated failure: 2 failed turns, OR the customer rephrases the same question Regulated topic: dispute, complaint, closing an account, source of funds, a customer who says they are vulnerable Asks for a person: any wording

Mark when each one is checked. Human-tier intents fire on the first message: the map already said a person handles them. The other four are checked on every customer turn, because they only become visible as the conversation goes on. Write "every turn" next to them in so many words, or someone will build them as a first-message filter.

Define the packet. Copy the seven fields. For each one, name where it comes from (the transcript, the classifier, the verification log, the account system), because a field nobody populates is a field the agent re-asks.

Write the no-re-asking rule. One sentence, aimed at agents, not at the bot: the agent does not ask for anything already in the packet. If the packet is wrong or empty, that is a defect report, not a reason to start over with the customer.

Write what the bot says while it hands over. Short, honest about what happens next, and never a promise about money or policy it cannot keep. After Moffatt, the hand-over message is the one sentence you should assume will be quoted back to you.

★ The hand-over message "I'm passing you to a colleague now. They can see this whole conversation, so you won't need to repeat anything. Typical wait: [live estimate]." Never: "Your refund is approved", "You're eligible", "That fee will be waived".

Name an owner. Triggers drift: someone adds an intent, someone tunes a threshold to "improve containment". One named person signs off on any change to this section, and that person is not the vendor.

Preview what it is worth on session 6's bench. These are the bench's numbers, from constructed inputs; yours will differ. With the bot on "Automate the safe third", 735 customers a month are falsely contained. Add a hand-off that catches 70 percent of failing chats, the "+ hand-off" rung, and that falls to 221. The all-in cost per resolved contact, which counts the customers a false containment loses, moves from $6.98 to $6.61.

Bot fails. "Why can't I verify my identity?" is scored 0.68 as a harmless explainer. The bot explains why banks verify.

Trigger. The customer writes "It is impossible to verify my identity". Same question, rephrased: repeated failure fires.

Packet. Both messages verbatim, the 0.68, the bot's answer, what was verified, and "repeated failure" as the reason.

Agent. Opens with "I can see the check failed twice", not "How can I help?".

Resolved. One hand-off, no repeat explanation, and the customer never learns how close they came to a loop.

Bot: "Let me connect you to an agent."

Agent: "Hi, thanks for waiting! How can I help you today? Could you confirm your email and describe the problem?" The customer explains for the third time.

Bot: "I'm passing you to a colleague who can see this conversation."

Agent: "I can see your identity check failed twice and you've already confirmed your email and phone. Let me look at why." The customer explains nothing again.

Why the hand-off counts as a win, not a failure. A dashboard built on containment scores every hand-off as a loss. Session 1's definition does not: a handed-off customer who is resolved by a person is resolved. On session 6's bench, adding the hand-off lowers the vendor-reported resolution rate and lowers the all-in cost per resolved contact at the same time. Both numbers are true; only one describes the customers.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Find your own loop. Pull 20 bot conversations where the customer wrote three or more times. Count how many show the same question asked twice before any hand-off. That count is what your repeated-failure trigger would have caught.
  2. Audit one real hand-off. Read one conversation from the moment the bot hands over to the agent's first line. List every question the agent asked that the conversation had already answered. Each one is a packet field you are missing.
  3. Read the hand-over line out loud. Find what your bot says when it hands over. Ask of it: does it promise anything about money or policy? If yes, rewrite it tonight.
  4. Read Moffatt in full. It is short. Note the one sentence you would put in front of whoever approves your bot's policy answers, and why.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · "Why can't I verify my identity?" is scored 0.68 as the harmless intent why verify identity. Which trigger catches it?

The intent trigger never sees the true intent, because the classifier got it wrong with confidence. A gate high enough to stop a 0.68 would strand most safe messages too. The rephrased second message is the evidence, which is why the checks run on every turn.

2 · In Moffatt v. Air Canada, what did the tribunal decide about the chatbot, and why does it matter even though it is a small-claims decision?

The tribunal called the separate-entity argument "a remarkable submission" and found negligent misrepresentation, awarding $812.02. It is a British Columbia small-claims decision, not binding precedent elsewhere, and still the plainest statement of the principle on record.

3 · What does the context packet prevent?

The packet travels to the agent, not back to the bot. Its whole job is that nobody asks twice: the customer's words, what was said, what was verified and why the hand-off fired arrive with the chat.
