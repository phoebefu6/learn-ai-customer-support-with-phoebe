LiveWhy reading beats every number you already have3 min▶

Go back to rows 14 and 17 of session 1's log. One customer got the steps for a first-time identity check when theirs kept failing. The other got instructions for ordering a virtual card when theirs would not work. Neither came back within 7 days. Containment counted both as wins, the repeat-contact window counted both as resolved, and neither customer was asked how it went.

The only instrument that saw them was a person reading the conversation. Tonight is about making that reading systematic, cheap enough to do every week, and honest about what it can and cannot see.

Part 1 · covers sampling

A silent failure is only found by reading it

6 min live

You cannot read every conversation, and you do not need to. You need a sample drawn so that the conversations most likely to hurt someone are the ones most likely to be read. That means stratifying by route and over-sampling the risky intents, then feeding the sample to one rubric and to two kinds of reader.

LiveStratify by route, then over-sample what can hurt4 min▶

A plain random sample of Quayside's week would be mostly balance questions and card activations, because that is what most conversations are. You would read dozens of easy wins and, in a typical week, very few of the conversations where a wrong answer costs a customer money. So the sample is drawn in layers.

LayerWhat it catchesWhy it gets its own quota
Automate routeWrong answers the customer accepted, like rows 14 and 17Nobody else ever looks at these conversations
Assist routeA draft the agent sent without fixing itSession 3's rules only work if someone checks they were followed
Human routeHand-offs that arrived late or without the context packetSession 4's triggers fire in this layer, or fail to

Within the automate layer, over-sample the risky intents: the ones that sit nearest the line on session 2's map, where a misread intent sends money questions to the bot. If those are a small share of volume, a proportional sample would hardly ever draw one. Over-sampling means you deliberately read more of them than their share, then weight them back down when you report a rate for the whole route.

Two numbers, never confused. The sample's failure rate on risky intents tells you where to look. The weighted rate across the route tells you how the route is doing. Reporting the over-sampled rate as if it were the route's rate makes the bot look worse than it is, and the first time someone notices, the whole QA plan loses its credibility.

Real world

Zendesk's resolution tiers, introduced on May 18, 2026, include a verified tier in which a large language model "evaluates the text of the conversation to confirm that the customer's request was satisfactorily resolved". That is automated QA, run by the vendor, on the vendor's own bot. It is a useful signal and a sensible design. It is also exactly the kind of judge that Part 2 says must be checked against people before anyone treats its verdict as final.

Self-studyHow big a sample, honestly3 min▶

There is no published standard for how many support conversations to read a week, and any number on a vendor slide is a sales choice. What decides it in practice is reviewer time and how rare the failure you are hunting is. If a failure appears in one conversation in fifty, a weekly read of twenty will often contain none of them, and a quiet week will look like a good week.

So size the sample from the other end: how many hours can your QA reviewers give each week, how long does one careful read take, and which layer can you least afford to miss? The build-along sets Quayside's constructed numbers this way. Treat them as a starting shape, not a benchmark.

Part 2 · covers the LLM judge and one rubric

One test for the bot and the people, and a judge that earns its seat

5 min live

If the bot is graded on whether the customer left and the agents are graded on a ten-point QA form, you have built a comparison the bot cannot lose. One rubric, applied to both, is the only way "the bot is as good as our people" can ever be a fair sentence. And a judge that scores at scale is worth having only once you know it agrees with the people it replaces.

LiveThe five criteria, and why each one is there3 min▶
CriterionThe question the reader answersWhat it catches that a dashboard cannot
ResolvedDid the customer's actual problem end?Rows 14 and 17: the chat ended, the problem did not
CorrectWas every fact, fee, limit and step right?A confident wrong answer the customer believed
Policy-compliantDid it stay inside what Quayside allows, and hand off when it should?A promise nobody is authorised to make
Effort for the customerHow many times did they have to repeat themselves?The loop session 4 was written to stop
ToneWould you be comfortable reading it aloud to the customer?The reply that was right and still lost them

The same five go on every conversation, whichever route it took. That is what makes "the bot resolves as well as our people" a claim you can test rather than a slide you have to believe.

Real world

The largest field study of AI in a support team, Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's Generative AI at Work (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025), found that with an assist tool "the most experienced and highest-skilled workers see small gains in speed and small declines in quality". You only see a decline like that if people's conversations are graded too. A rubric that only grades the bot would have missed half the story.

LiveWhen the judge's score can stand without a person2 min▶

An LLM judge is an instrument. Like any instrument it is trusted after it has been checked against a known reference, and only for as long as nothing has changed. Three conditions, all of them, before Quayside lets a judge's score stand alone:

  • It agrees with people on a calibration set. Reviewers and the judge label the same conversations against the same rubric, and the agreement clears a bar Quayside wrote down in advance.
  • It is re-checked on a schedule, and again whenever the bot, the rubric or the judge changes. A new model version is a new instrument.
  • It never grades its own vendor's bot unchecked. A vendor's judge scoring a vendor's bot is a reasonable signal and a conflict of interest at the same time. Calibrate it like any other judge, against your own reviewers.

How to build a judge prompt, measure agreement properly and assemble the golden set it is calibrated on is taught in full elsewhere and not repeated here: AI Evals: the LLM judge ↗, AI Evals: the golden set ↗, and Agent Launch: the judge as an instrument ↗.

Self-studyWhen the judge and the reviewer disagree, who wins3 min▶

During calibration, the person's label stands. That is not because people are always right; reviewers disagree with each other too. It is because the rubric was written for people to apply, and the judge is being checked against that use of it. A useful habit: when the two disagree, read the conversation together and ask whether the rubric was clear. Often the fix is a sharper criterion, not a better judge.

After calibration, keep a small human read every week anyway. It is the tripwire that tells you the judge has drifted before the next scheduled re-check does.

Part 3 · covers CSAT and the contested case

The survey hears only from the customers who answer it

6 min live

CSAT is the number every bot vendor reaches for, because it is easy to collect and it sounds like the customer's own verdict. It is the verdict of the customers who chose to answer. The falsely contained customer who never came back never answers the survey either.

LiveWho answers a survey, and what the evidence does and does not show2 min▶

The solid evidence is from healthcare, not support. Tyser and colleagues (BMC Health Services Research, 2016) studied a patient satisfaction survey at a medical practice. "The survey response rate was 16.5 %", and the survey "exhibited non-response bias": the odds of answering varied with age, sex, insurance type and subspecialty. What that shows is that who answers differs from who does not. It does not measure how the non-respondents felt.

The support evidence is a preprint. Hong and Potteiger (arXiv, July 2026, not peer reviewed) report "a full-census 2.91 against the surveyed 3.62 on a five-point scale" at one firm. The 2.91 is itself an LLM's estimate over about 9,000 conversations, not a survey of everyone. It is suggestive, and it points the way you would expect. It is not a finding to put in a board pack.

What to take from both. You do not need proof that non-respondents are less happy to stop treating CSAT as the whole story. You only need to know that respondents are a self-selected group, and that the customers a bot failed most quietly are the least likely to be in it.

LiveKlarna: three statements, three different measurements4 min▶

Klarna is the most quoted support-bot story there is, and it is usually told as either a triumph or a reversal. Read the sources side by side and it is neither. It is a disagreement about what was being measured, and it is not settled.

Real world

Side one: the launch, 27 February 2024. Klarna's press release, read at source, reported 2.3 million conversations in the first month, "two-thirds of Klarna's customer service chats", "the equivalent work of 700 full-time agents" (a workload estimate, not a headcount of people let go), customer satisfaction "on par with human agents", "a 25% drop in repeat inquiries", resolution in "less than 2 mins compared to 11 mins previously", and an estimated $40 million profit improvement in 2024.

Real world

Side two: the CEO, May 2025, as reported. CX Dive, quoting Bloomberg, reported Sebastian Siemiatkowski saying: "As cost unfortunately seems to have been a too predominant evaluation factor when organizing this, what you end up having is lower quality", and "Really investing in the quality of the human support is the way of the future for us."

Real world

Side three: the company, then and later. A spokesperson told CX Dive: "The chatbot still handles two-thirds of all customer inquiries." and "AI gives us speed. Talent gives us empathy." On the Q3 2025 earnings call, as reported by CX Dive, the assistant could do the work of more than 853 full-time agents and had saved $60 million.

The lesson is not who is right. Side one and side three measured workload, speed and CSAT. Side two talked about quality. Every one of those statements can be true at once, which is exactly the problem: an organisation that reports one kind of number can be surprised by the other. That is why Quayside's QA plan reports a sampled read, a true resolution rate and CSAT with its response rate, never one of them alone.

For how the same story looks at launch time, before any of this was known, see Agent Launch: why bots fail ↗.

Self-studyThe one Klarna number that speaks to resolution2 min▶

Of everything in the 2024 release, "a 25% drop in repeat inquiries" is the only figure that speaks to whether problems ended, rather than where chats ended or how fast. It is also the only one that could, in principle, be checked against a sampled read: a repeat inquiry is a customer telling you the first answer did not work.

Notice what it cannot show. A customer who got a wrong answer and gave up does not make a repeat inquiry. Session 1 called that false containment, and tonight's sample is the instrument built to find it.

Build-along

Quayside's QA plan, one weekly loop

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You write section 5 of Quayside's support operating plan: who gets read, against what, by whom, how the survey is reported, and where every failure goes next. Every number below is a constructed Quayside example. Your numbers will differ, and the shape of the plan is the part worth copying.

Set the weekly sample, per route. Quayside handles about 14,000 contacts a month. Its constructed weekly sample is 150 conversations, split by route, with the risky automate-tier intents read at more than their share. When you report a rate for a whole route, weight those back down.

★ The weekly sample (constructed) Automate route 80 conversations, of which 30 from the risky intents nearest the line on the session 2 map Assist route 40 conversations where a person sent an AI draft Human route 30 conversations, every one a hand-off from the bot Total 150 a week. Your numbers will differ.

Write the rubric once, for everyone. Five criteria, each a yes or no with a one-line reason, applied to bot and human conversations alike. A failure on any of the first three fails the conversation.

★ The rubric 1 Resolved did the customer's actual problem end? 2 Correct was every fact, fee, limit and step right? 3 Policy-compliant inside what Quayside allows; handed off when required? 4 Effort did the customer have to repeat themselves? 5 Tone could you read it aloud to the customer? Fail on 1, 2 or 3 = a failed read. 4 and 5 are reported, not failed.

Write the judge calibration rule. The bar below is Quayside's own choice, written before the first calibration run so nobody can move it afterwards. It is not an industry standard; there is none.

★ Judge calibration (constructed bar) Before the judge grades alone: reviewers and judge label the same 100 conversations. The judge must match the reviewers on criteria 1 to 3 in at least 90 of 100. Until then, people grade and the judge's labels are logged only. Re-check: monthly, and whenever the bot, the rubric or the judge model changes. A vendor's own judge is calibrated the same way before its score is reported under Quayside's name. Reviewers still read 40 of the 150 every week, judge or no judge.

Write the CSAT rule. CSAT stays in the report, because customers who answer are telling you something real. It just never travels alone.

★ The CSAT rule CSAT is always reported with its response rate, in the same line. CSAT is never reported alone, and never as the resolution number. Bot and human CSAT are compared only alongside the rubric results for the same routes.

Route every failed read to an owner. A failed read is a finding about an earlier section of the plan. Name which one, so the fix lands with the person who owns it.

★ Where failures go Wrong route, the bot should never have answered -> owner of the map (section 2) A person sent a draft that was wrong -> owner of the assist rules (section 3) Handed off too late, or without the context packet -> owner of the hand-off rules (section 4) Each owner replies in writing within the week. The fix is checked in next week's sample.

Assemble the section. Copy this into section 5 of your version of Quayside's plan. Change any number you can defend changing; keep every rule.

★ Plan section 5 · the QA plan Sample: 150 conversations a week, stratified by route (automate 80, assist 40, human 30), risky intents over-sampled and weighted back for route-level rates. Constructed; set from reviewer hours. Rubric: resolved, correct, policy-compliant, customer effort, tone. One rubric for bot and people. Fail on 1, 2 or 3 = a failed read. Judge: grades alone only after matching reviewers in 90 of 100 on criteria 1 to 3; re-checked monthly and on any change to bot, rubric or judge. Reviewers read 40 a week regardless. CSAT: always with its response rate, never alone, never as the resolution number. Weekly line: containment, true resolution, false containment, failed reads by route, CSAT with response rate. Every failed read goes to the owner of section 2, 3 or 4. Owner of this section: the QA lead.

CSAT 4.4 of 5. Bot and agents level.

Bot conversations rated as highly as people this week. Customers are happy with the assistant. No action needed before next month's review.

CSAT 4.4 of 5, from 9% who answered. 150 read, 12 failed.

Failed reads routed: 7 to the map, 3 to the assist rules, 2 to the hand-off. Judge matched reviewers this month. Owners reply by Friday.

Sample. 150 conversations drawn by route, the risky intents read at more than their share. Constructed.

Rubric. Every one is graded on the same five criteria, bot or person.

Judge. The LLM judge labels all 150 against the rubric.

Calibrate. Reviewers label 40 of the same. Where they disagree, the person's label stands and the rubric gets sharper.

Report. The weekly line, CSAT beside its response rate, and every failed read sent to its owner.

Why 12 failures in 150 is good news. Every one of them is a conversation you found before the customer had to tell you, with an owner who can fix the cause. A QA report with zero failures in a week usually means the sample was too small or the rubric too gentle, not that the bot was perfect.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Read 20 of your own conversations against the five criteria. Draw them by route if you can. Count how many fail on resolved, correct or policy-compliant, and note which of them your dashboard counted as resolved.
  2. Find your CSAT response rate. If your CSAT report does not print it, ask for it. Then write the two in one line: "CSAT X from Y percent who answered".
  3. Check who grades your bot. If a vendor's own model scores it, find out whether anyone has compared those scores with a person's on the same conversations, and when that was last done.
  4. Route three failures. Take three failed reads from step 1 and write next to each the section of the plan it belongs to: the map, the assist rules or the hand-off. If one fits none of them, that is a gap in the plan worth naming.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The bot's CSAT matches the agents' this month. Why is that not enough to say the bot is resolving as well as people?

Respondents are a self-selected group; Tyser 2016 showed who answers differs, in healthcare. The customers a bot failed most quietly are the least likely to be in that group, so CSAT is reported with its response rate and beside a sampled read, never alone.

2 · When can Quayside report an LLM judge's scores without a person checking them?

A judge is an instrument: trusted after it is checked against people on the same conversations, and checked again on a schedule and whenever anything it depends on changes. A vendor's judge grading the vendor's bot is calibrated the same way.

3 · Klarna's 2024 release reported CSAT on par with agents; in 2025 its CEO was reported saying cost-focus led to lower quality. What is the disagreement actually about?

Nothing in the sources says the 2024 figures were false, and the spokesperson said the chatbot "still handles two-thirds of all customer inquiries". The sides measured different things, which is why a QA plan reports more than one number.
