LiveWhy the map comes before the bot3 min▶

Session 1 showed that a bot's resolution rate is lower than its containment rate. Part of the gap is the bot answering badly. A bigger part, usually, is the bot answering at all: messages that should never have reached it. That is a triage decision, and it is made before any model is chosen.

Triage here means routing tickets to queues and people. Routing requests between models to save cost is a different problem, taught in LLM Inference: routing ↗.

Part 1 · covers intent, urgency and risk

Three readings of one message

6 min live

Intent is what the customer wants. Urgency is how fast it has to happen. Risk is what it costs if the answer is wrong. A classifier gives you the first; the other two are decisions your organisation makes per intent, and they are the ones that decide who answers.

Real world

Another real message from the same test set: "Why can't I verify my identity?" The playground's classifier scores it 0.68 against why verify identity, a harmless explainer intent, when its true label is unable to verify identity, a customer locked out of their own money. The words are almost the same. The risk is not. You will meet this message again in the build-along.

Self-studyWhat Banking77 is, and what it is not4 min▶

Banking77 was released by PolyAI with the paper "Efficient Intent Detection with Dual Sentence Encoders" (Casanueva, Temcinas, Gerz, Henderson and Vulic, 2020). Its dataset card describes "13,083 customer service queries labeled with 77 intents"; the repository splits them into 10,003 training and 3,080 test examples. It is licensed CC BY 4.0.

The playground uses 24 training messages and 8 test messages per intent, drawn with a fixed seed, so 1,848 to learn from and 616 to triage. The text is unchanged. Three honest limits: every message is a single short query, not a conversation; the test set is balanced at the same count per intent, which real queues never are; and the risk tier of each intent is Quayside's constructed judgement, not part of the data.

Part 2 · covers the automate, assist and human map

Volume tells you where the work is, risk tells you who does it

6 min live

Each intent gets one of three routes. Automate: the bot answers and the conversation may end without a person. Assist: a person answers, with an AI draft beside them (session 3). Human: a person answers, and the bot's job is to get the customer there fast with the context attached (session 4).

LiveThree tests for every intent, in this order4 min▶
  1. Risk first. If a wrong answer can lose the customer money, expose their identity, or touch a regulated process (disputes, complaints, closing an account, source of funds), the route is human. Volume does not change this.
  2. Answerability second. If answering needs the customer's own account (where is my card, why is my transfer pending), the bot cannot answer from the help centre. The route is assist, unless the bot is genuinely connected to that data and you have tested it.
  3. Volume last. Among what survives the first two tests, automate the busiest first, because that is where the savings are.

Applied to Quayside's 77 intents this gives 36 automate, 29 assist and 12 human. In the balanced test sample, 46.8 percent of messages belong to an automate intent. That is the ceiling on safe coverage before the classifier makes a single mistake.

Real world

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's study of AI assistance for 5,000-plus support agents reports that "the gains from AI adoption are largest for moderately rare problems, where human agents have less baseline experience but the system still has adequate training data" (QJE 2025 abstract). Rare intents are poor automation candidates and good assist candidates: the person still answers, with help. Session 3 builds on it.

Self-studyWhen a message carries two intents3 min▶

Real messages often do. "Transactions on my card look really weird. I've been charged twice for a couple things this week! Please check this and return the double charged ones." (a Banking77 test message) is labelled transaction charged twice, but "look really weird" is the start of a fraud report. A single-label classifier returns one intent. The safe rule is that the riskiest plausible reading sets the route: if any reading is human, the message goes to a person.

The playground cannot show this, because every Banking77 message carries exactly one label. Say so when you present it.

Part 3 · covers reading the confusion

An average accuracy hides where the mistakes land

5 min live

The playground's classifier matches 481 of 616 messages to the right intent. That one number says nothing about which 135 it got wrong, and for triage that is the only thing that matters: a mistake between two harmless intents costs a repeat contact, a mistake into an automate intent can cost a customer their money.

LiveThe busiest-intents plan fails with a correct classifier3 min▶

Rank Banking77's intents by how many training examples each has (a stand-in for volume) and the top 20 include five that Quayside routes to a person: card payment not recognised, cash withdrawal not recognised, direct debit payment not recognised, transaction charged twice, and wrong amount of cash received. Disputes are among the most common things bank customers write about.

Automate those 20 and the bot answers 37 risky messages. Read them in the playground: most are classified correctly. "There is a card payment I do not recognise." is matched to card payment not recognised with a score of 0.645. The classifier did its job. The map sent a fraud report to a help article.

Real world

Gartner predicted in March 2025 that "By 2029, agentic AI will autonomously resolve 80% of common customer service issues without human intervention". It is a prediction, not a finding, and the word doing the work is "common". In a bank's inbox, common and safe are different lists.

Self-studyThe confusions, read as pairs3 min▶

The most frequent confusions in the 616 are near-synonyms: top up reverted read as top up failed (3 times), declined cash withdrawal read as declined transfer or pending cash withdrawal (2 each), get disposable virtual card read as getting virtual card (2). Per queue, Fraud and loss is matched right 48 of 56 times and Transfers 61 of 88.

Pairs like these are why the map should be drawn on groups of intents that share a route, not on single intents. A confusion inside one route costs a slightly wrong answer. A confusion that crosses from human into automate costs the customer.

Build-along

The triage playground: 616 real messages

★ 22 min · everyone builds

When this page loaded, your browser trained a classifier on 1,848 real Banking77 messages and ran it on 616 more. Nothing is simulated: every count below is computed from real text. What is constructed is Quayside's risk tier per intent, and the playground labels that.

Read the classifier line first. 481 of 616 messages matched to the right intent, 78.1 percent. Keep that number in mind as the thing that does NOT change while you change the map: every preset below runs on the same predictions.

Click "Automate everything". Coverage 100 percent, 135 bot answers to the wrong intent (21.9 percent of them), and 96 risky messages kept from a person. This is a bot with no triage at all.

Click "Automate the busiest 20". Coverage drops to 24.5 percent, and the risky count is still 37. Open "Read the messages the bot would have answered wrongly", then open the intent list and find the five human-tier intents sitting in the busiest 20.

Click "Risk-first map". Quayside's constructed tiers: coverage 48.1 percent, 4 risky, and 62 wrong-intent answers (20.9 percent of bot answers). More coverage than the busiest 20 and a fraction of the risk, because the map was drawn on risk before volume.

Click "Risk-first + confidence gate". The bot answers only when its score is at least 0.40. Coverage 33.9 percent, wrong-intent answers fall to 29 (13.9 percent), and 88 safe messages now reach a person. Drag the slider: at 0.30 coverage is 42.9 percent with 17.4 percent wrong, at 0.50 it is 17.9 percent with 12.7 percent wrong. There is no free setting.

★ The two risky messages the gate cannot stop "Why can't I verify my identity?" scored 0.68 as: why verify identity "It is impossible to verify my identity" scored 0.68 as: why verify identity True label for both: unable to verify identity (human)

Break it. Press "Break it: shuffle the training labels". The classifier now matches 3 of 616, and the gate passes almost nothing. If the bench could not get worse, it would not be measuring anything; this proves the numbers above come from what the classifier learned. Restore the labels.

★ Plan section 2 · the automate / assist / human map Tests, in order: risk (money, identity, regulated: human), then answerability (needs the account: assist), then volume. Automate: 36 informational intents, only when the classifier's score is at least 0.40. Assist: 29 intents that need the customer's account. Human: 12 intents (fraud, disputes, identity, closing an account). Measured on 616 real messages: 33.9% answered by the bot, 29 to the wrong intent, 2 risky. Those 2 are the hand-off's job.

What the playground deliberately leaves out. Real message volumes, conversations longer than one message, and anything a customer writes after the first answer. Those are where the next three sessions live: the assist draft, the hand-off trigger, and the QA read.

Homework

Before session 3

30 min
  1. List your own top 20 contact reasons by volume. From your ticketing tool's tags, or from a sample of 100 tickets if the tags cannot be trusted.
  2. Run the three tests on each one: risk, then answerability, then volume. Mark every reason that is in your top 20 by volume and fails the risk test.
  3. Find your two near-synonyms. Two contact reasons whose wording is almost the same and whose risk is opposite, like "why verify identity" and "unable to verify identity". Write the words that tell them apart.
  4. Decide your gate. If you had to choose between 42.9 percent coverage with 17.4 percent wrong answers and 33.9 percent with 13.9 percent, which would you sign, and what would you need from session 4 to sign the other?
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Automating Banking77's 20 busiest intents keeps 37 risky messages from a person, even though most of them are classified correctly. What went wrong?

Most of the 37 are correct classifications of risky intents. The fix is the order of the tests: risk first, then answerability, then volume.

2 · Raising the confidence gate on the risk-first map from 0 to 0.40 changes coverage from 48.1 to 33.9 percent. What does it buy?

Accuracy does not move, because the predictions are the same; the gate changes which predictions the bot acts on. Two risky messages are still scored 0.68, which no sensible gate catches.

3 · Shuffling the training labels drops the classifier to 3 of 616. Why is that button on the page?

Break the thing the result depends on and the result should collapse. It does, to about one in 77 by chance, which is what a classifier that learned nothing would score.
